Keyword Research Step by Step: From Seed Ideas to Priorities

A step-by-step keyword research process: find seed ideas, read search intent, weigh difficulty against opportunity, analyze the SERP, and prioritize topics.

GGrow.Media Editorial Team·Updated October 30, 2026·7 min read

Keyword research tells you what your audience actually types into search and which of those searches you have a realistic chance of winning. Done well, it turns a vague content plan into a ranked list of pages worth writing. This guide gives you a repeatable process, from the first brainstorm to a scored priority sheet.

Key takeaways

  • Start from your audience's problems, not from a tool. Tools expand ideas; they don't replace understanding your readers.
  • Search intent decides what kind of page can rank. Check the results page before you commit to a keyword.
  • Difficulty scores are third-party estimates. Real competitiveness shows up when you look at who actually ranks.
  • Group closely related keywords onto one page so your own pages don't compete with each other.
  • Score every candidate on value, intent fit, winnability, and effort, then work from the top of the list down.

Step 1: Gather seed ideas

Seeds are broad starting terms that describe your topic. Pull them from places where your audience already speaks in its own words:

  1. Audience questions: emails, comments, sales calls, support tickets, and direct messages.
  2. Your offer or expertise: problems you solve, features, use cases, and the alternatives people compare you with.
  3. Search engine suggestions: autocomplete, "People also ask" boxes, and related searches at the bottom of results pages.
  4. Communities: forums and social threads where your audience asks for help.
  5. Competitors: their navigation menus, category pages, and most-shared content.

Aim for 10 to 20 seeds. For a neighborhood bakery, seeds might include "sourdough bread," "gluten-free cake," "wedding cake," "bakery near me," and "how to store bread."

Step 2: Expand into a long list

Enter each seed into a keyword tool (many offer free tiers) and into search suggestions, then export everything into one spreadsheet. Record the estimated monthly volume, but don't treat it as precise. Tools often disagree substantially, so use volume to compare phrases rather than to predict traffic.

Pay special attention to long-tail keywords: longer, more specific phrases. Each one has lower volume, but together they make up a large share of all searches, and they tend to be easier to rank for and closer to a decision. "Sourdough starter smells like acetone" is a far more winnable first target for a new baking blog than "sourdough."

Step 3: Classify search intent

Intent is the job the searcher wants done. Classify each keyword by what currently ranks, not by what you assume.

Intent Example query Page type that usually ranks
Informational how to store sourdough bread Step-by-step guide, explainer, video
Commercial investigation best stand mixer for bread dough Comparison, review roundup, buyer's guide
Transactional order custom birthday cake online Product, service, or booking page
Navigational [brand name] opening hours The brand's own site or profile
Local bakery near me Map results and business profiles

If your page type doesn't match the dominant result type, either change the format you plan to publish or pick a different keyword. Fighting intent is one of the most common reasons good content never ranks.

Step 4: Analyze the search results page

Open the results for each promising keyword in a private browser window and work through this checklist:

  • Format: Are the top results guides, lists, tools, product pages, or videos?
  • Who ranks: Large brands, niche specialists, small blogs, or forums?
  • Freshness: Are the results recent, or several years old?
  • Depth: Do they fully answer the question, or leave obvious gaps?
  • Page features: Featured snippets, video carousels, map packs, and shopping results push regular listings down the page and reduce available clicks.

Signs of genuine opportunity include forum threads ranking in the top five (often a sign that no dedicated resource exists), outdated articles, small sites ranking alongside big ones, and results that only partially answer the query.

Step 5: Weigh difficulty against opportunity

Most tools assign a difficulty score, usually based largely on the links pointing to the ranking pages. Each tool calculates it differently, so use the score as a rough filter rather than a verdict. Your own site matters too: a new site should start with lower-competition terms and expand as it earns relevance and links.

Opportunity is a combination of volume, business value, and intent fit. A phrase searched 50 times a month by people ready to buy can be worth more than an informational phrase searched 5,000 times by casual browsers. Ask, "If I ranked first tomorrow, would this traffic matter to my goals?"

Step 6: Cluster related keywords

Many keywords share the same intent and are best answered by a single page. A quick test: search two keywords and compare the top ten results. If several of the same URLs appear for both, target them with one page. If the results are mostly different, they probably need separate pages.

For example, "how to store sourdough bread," "keep sourdough fresh," and "freeze a sourdough loaf" belong on one page. "Sourdough starter recipe" is a different job and deserves its own. Once you have clusters, you can organize them around pillar pages, as explained in the topic clusters guide.

Step 7: Score and prioritize

Rate each cluster from 1 to 3 on four criteria, then add up the scores:

  • Business value: How closely does this topic connect to what you offer?
  • Intent fit: Can you create the page type that ranks?
  • Winnability: How beatable are the current results?
  • Effort: How easy is it to produce? Score low effort as 3.
Keyword cluster Value Intent fit Winnability Effort Total
How to store sourdough 2 3 3 3 11
Custom wedding cakes [city] 3 3 2 2 10
Best stand mixer for bread 1 2 1 1 5
Gluten-free birthday cake ideas 3 3 2 2 10

Sort by total and start at the top. When two clusters tie, choose the one with higher business value. Re-score quarterly, because your winnability improves as your site gains authority.

Turning the list into content

Give each page one primary keyword and work its cluster variants in naturally through headings and answers. Place the main phrase near the front of your title, then check that it reads well and won't be cut off using the SERP snippet preview tool.

Avoid these common mistakes:

  1. Choosing keywords by volume alone and ignoring intent.
  2. Creating several thin pages for near-identical phrases.
  3. Copying the structure of the top result without adding anything new.
  4. Never checking Search Console after publishing. Give pages four to eight weeks, then review the queries they actually attract and expand the content to match.

Your next step

Open a blank spreadsheet and list 15 seed ideas drawn from real audience questions. Expand them into a long list, classify the intent of your top 20 candidates by checking the live results, cluster the overlaps, and score each cluster. Pick the top three and outline the first page this week. A short, well-prioritized list you actually publish beats a giant list that stays in a spreadsheet.

Frequently asked questions

How many keywords should one page target?

Each page should have one primary keyword plus the close variants and related questions that share the same intent. If two phrases return mostly the same top results, one page can usually serve both. If the results differ, they likely need separate pages.

Are search volume numbers from keyword tools accurate?

Treat them as rough estimates. Different tools often report noticeably different numbers for the same phrase, and low-volume terms are especially imprecise. Use volume to compare keywords against each other, not as a traffic forecast.

Should a new website target high-volume keywords?

Usually not at first. New sites tend to have better odds with specific long-tail phrases where the current results are weak, outdated, or dominated by forums. Winning those builds the relevance and links that make broader terms realistic later.

How often should I redo keyword research?

Do a full pass when you plan a new content theme, and a lighter review every quarter. Search Console will also surface new queries you already appear for, which are often the fastest opportunities to act on.
