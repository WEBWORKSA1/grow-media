How to Build a Media Kit That Wins Sponsors and Brand Deals

Learn how to build a media kit that wins sponsors: what audience data to include, how to package and price offers, and a one-page template outline to copy.

GGrow.Media Editorial Team·Updated October 30, 2026·8 min read
On this pageKey takeawaysWhat a media kit is for (and what it is not)The essential sectionsPackaging your offersPricing and how to present itThe one-page media kit templateCommon mistakes to avoidYour next step

A media kit is the document a sponsor opens when they are deciding whether you are worth their budget. It needs to answer three questions fast: who is your audience, what can you do for the brand, and what does it cost. This guide shows you exactly what to include, how to present it, and a one-page outline you can build today.

Key takeaways

  • A media kit is a sales document, so lead with the brand's problem and your audience fit, not your life story.
  • Audience quality (who they are, where they live, how engaged they are) usually matters more than follower counts.
  • Package your offers into two to four clear tiers instead of an open-ended menu.
  • Show proof with real past results, screenshots, or collaborations you can verify.
  • Keep it to one page, date it, and update the numbers every quarter.

What a media kit is for (and what it is not)

Think of a media kit as the bridge between a cold pitch and a signed contract. Brand managers and agencies review many creators at once, often skimming. Your kit should let them decide in under a minute whether to start a conversation.

It is not a résumé, a portfolio of everything you have ever made, or a place for vague claims. Every element should help a buyer answer one question: will this partnership reach the people we want and move them to act?

There are two broad types:

Type Typical owner Main selling point
Creator media kit YouTubers, podcasters, social creators Personal trust and audience engagement
Publisher media kit Newsletters, blogs, niche sites Targeted traffic and placement inventory

The structure is similar for both. Publishers lean more on traffic and placement specs; creators lean more on personality and audience relationship.

The essential sections

1. A one-line positioning statement

State who you serve and what they get from you. For example: "Practical weeknight cooking for busy parents, published twice a week." A buyer should immediately see whether your audience matches their customer.

2. Audience data

This is the heart of the kit. Include:

  • Size by platform: followers, subscribers, or list size for each channel you sell.
  • Reach: average views per video, average impressions per post, or average opens per email. Brands increasingly care more about this than follower totals.
  • Engagement: engagement rate, average comments, click-through rate on links. Calculate it consistently with the engagement rate calculator so your numbers are defensible.
  • Demographics: top countries, age ranges, and gender split from your platform analytics.
  • Interests and intent: what your audience is trying to do or buy, drawn from comments, surveys, and the topics that perform best.

Always pull numbers from native analytics, note the time period (for example, "last 90 days"), and round honestly. Inflated numbers get discovered once a campaign runs, and that ends repeat business.

3. Content formats and placements

List exactly what a brand can buy: a dedicated video, an integrated mention, a newsletter sponsor slot, a story sequence, a social post, or a bundle. For each, note length, placement, and what is included, such as a link, a discount code, or usage rights.

4. Proof and past results

Show logos of past partners only with permission. Better still, add short, factual results you can back up, such as "Integrated mention: 18,000 views, 2.1% link click-through, 30-day window." If you have no sponsors yet, use organic proof: a top-performing piece of content, a product you recommended that readers bought, or survey results from your audience.

5. Packages and pricing

Covered in detail below.

6. Contact and next step

Your name, business email, preferred booking lead time, and one clear call to action, such as "Reply with your campaign dates and goals for a custom quote."

Packaging your offers

Brands buy outcomes, not line items. Bundling makes your offer easier to approve and raises average deal size.

A simple three-tier structure works for most creators:

Package What is included Best for
Starter One integrated mention or single sponsor slot Brands testing you for the first time
Standard Dedicated piece of content plus one social post Product launches and awareness
Partner Multi-month series, recurring mentions, usage rights Brands seeking ongoing association

Follow these rules when building tiers:

  1. Make the middle tier the one you most want to sell, and price the others around it.
  2. Add value through extras that cost you little, such as a pinned comment or a link in your bio for a week.
  3. Charge separately for usage rights, exclusivity, and whitelisting (letting the brand run your content as ads). These have real value and are often under-priced.
  4. Set minimum lead times and revision limits inside each package.

Pricing and how to present it

Most creators price from a combination of reach and engagement, then adjust for niche, format, and rights. Rates vary widely by niche; audiences in finance, software, and business tend to command higher rates than broad entertainment audiences because their purchases are worth more to advertisers.

A practical method:

  1. Start with your average views or opens per piece, not your follower count.
  2. Apply a CPM-style rate appropriate to your niche to get a baseline.
  3. Adjust up for high engagement, strong conversion history, or a specialist audience.
  4. Add fees for usage rights, exclusivity windows, and extra deliverables.
  5. Sanity-check the result against what the brand could earn from your audience.

The sponsorship rate calculator runs this math for you, and our guide on how to price brand deals covers negotiation in depth.

When presenting price, "starting at" figures work well. They anchor expectations, filter out mismatched budgets, and leave room for custom scopes. Avoid listing a dozen individual prices; it invites line-by-line haggling.

The one-page media kit template

Use this outline top to bottom. Each block should be short enough to read in seconds.

  1. Header: your name or brand, logo, a photo if you are the face of the channel, and the date updated.
  2. Positioning line: who you serve and what they get.
  3. Headline stats row: three to four key numbers, such as total audience, average views, engagement rate, and email open rate.
  4. Audience snapshot: top countries, age range, and two or three audience interests.
  5. Formats offered: a short list of placements with one-line descriptions.
  6. Packages: your three tiers with starting prices or "contact for pricing."
  7. Proof: two to three results or partner logos with permission.
  8. Contact: email, booking lead time, and one call to action.

Design tips: use one or two brand colors, large numbers for stats, and plenty of white space. Export to PDF and check that it reads clearly on a phone screen, since many buyers open attachments on mobile.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Leading with vanity metrics. Total followers without reach or engagement data looks like you are hiding something.
  • No dates on data. Undated numbers look stale even when they are not.
  • Too long. If it runs to many pages, buyers skim past the important parts.
  • Unverifiable claims. Phrases like "highly engaged audience" mean nothing without a number behind them.
  • No call to action. Tell the reader exactly what to send you next.

Your next step

Open your platform analytics and export the last 90 days of reach, engagement, and audience demographics. Draft the eight template blocks above in a plain document before worrying about design. Then run your average reach through the sponsorship rate calculator to set starting prices for your three packages, and send the finished kit to your first five target brands this week.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a media kit if my audience is small?

Yes, and arguably more so. A clear media kit shows a brand that you are organized and that your audience is specific, which matters more to many sponsors than raw size. Smaller creators often win deals on niche focus and engagement rather than reach.

Should I put my prices in my media kit?

It depends on how you sell. Listing starting prices filters out brands that cannot afford you and speeds up deals, while leaving prices off gives you room to scope custom work. A common middle ground is to show starting-at prices for each package and quote final numbers on request.

How often should I update my media kit?

Refresh the numbers at least once a quarter, and immediately after any major change such as a big growth spurt or a new platform. Outdated stats damage trust quickly. Keep a dated version so brands know the data is current.

What format should my media kit be in?

A one-page PDF is the most widely accepted format because it is easy to attach, forward, and print. Many creators also keep a web page version they can link to and update without resending files. Whatever you choose, make sure it reads well on a phone.
