Prompting for Marketers: Templates for Ads, Emails and Briefs

Practical prompting for marketers: the role, context, constraints and examples pattern, plus prompt templates for ads, emails, briefs and analysis.

GGrow.Media Editorial Team·Updated October 30, 2026·8 min read
On this pageKey takeawaysThe RCTCE patternPrompt templates for core marketing tasksIterate instead of restartingEvaluating AI outputGuardrails to build into every promptYour next step

AI assistants can draft ad variations, outline emails, and summarize campaign data in seconds, but only if you tell them what you actually need. Most disappointing output comes from vague prompts, not weak tools. This guide gives you a repeatable prompt pattern, copy-ready templates for common marketing tasks, and a simple way to judge what comes back.

Key takeaways

  • Brief an AI assistant like a skilled freelancer: role, context, task, constraints, and examples.
  • Specific inputs (audience, offer, proof, voice) are the biggest lever on output quality.
  • Ask for multiple distinct options, then iterate on the best one rather than starting over.
  • Score outputs against a short rubric before you use them.
  • Never let a model invent facts, statistics, testimonials, or claims about your product.

The RCTCE pattern

Strong marketing prompts share five parts. You can remember them as RCTCE: Role, Context, Task, Constraints, Examples.

Part Question it answers Example
Role Who should the assistant act as? "You are a direct-response copywriter for B2B software."
Context What does it need to know? Audience, product, offer, proof points, brand voice
Task What exactly should it produce? "Write five headline options for a landing page."
Constraints What are the rules? Length, tone, banned words, no invented claims, format
Examples What does good look like? One or two samples of copy you like

The context and constraints sections do the most work. A role line helps set tone, but without context the assistant still has to guess about your audience and offer.

Build a reusable context block

Rather than retyping background every time, keep a short context block you paste into every prompt: your audience description, a product summary, three to five verified proof points, voice adjectives, and words to avoid. Update it whenever your offer changes. This one habit makes outputs more consistent across tasks and team members, and it reduces the chance that the assistant fills gaps with invented details.

Before and after

Weak prompt: "Write a Facebook ad for my bakery."

Strong prompt:

You are a social media copywriter for local food businesses. My bakery is in a mid-sized town and sells sourdough and pastries. The audience is local parents aged roughly 30 to 50 who want a weekend treat. The offer is a free pastry with any loaf on Saturday mornings this month. Our voice is warm, simple, and slightly playful. Write four ad variations, each under 125 characters of primary text, each using a different angle: family ritual, freshness, the free pastry, and supporting a local business. Do not invent reviews, awards, or ingredients I have not mentioned.

The second prompt takes a minute longer to write and saves several rounds of editing.

Prompt templates for core marketing tasks

Copy these templates into your prompt library and fill the brackets.

Ad copy

Act as a performance marketer writing for [platform]. Product: [product]. Audience: [who, pain point, desired outcome]. Offer: [offer and deadline]. Proof I can use: [real facts only]. Write [number] variations of [primary text/headline] under [length]. Use these angles: [angle 1, angle 2, angle 3]. Return a table with columns: angle, headline, primary text, call to action. Do not add claims beyond the information given.

Email

You are an email marketer for [brand], whose voice is [three adjectives]. Write a [type: welcome, launch, re-engagement] email to [segment]. Goal: get them to [single action]. Key points: [bullets]. Constraints: subject line under 50 characters, three subject line options, preview text under 90 characters, body under [word count], one link only, plain-text friendly. End with a clear call to action.

Creative or content brief

Act as a marketing strategist. Create a one-page brief for [campaign or piece of content]. Include: objective, target audience, key message, three supporting points, required deliverables, channels, success metric, and deadline. Use this information: [notes]. Flag any information that is missing instead of assuming it.

That final instruction, asking the assistant to flag gaps, is useful in almost every template.

Data analysis

You are a marketing analyst. Below is campaign data in CSV format: [paste anonymized data]. Calculate CTR, CVR, CPA, and ROAS for each row and show your formulas. Identify the two best and two worst performers and explain the likely reasons. List three tests I could run next. If the data is insufficient to draw a conclusion, say so.

Always verify the arithmetic yourself or in a spreadsheet, because language models can make calculation errors. If you need a refresher on the formulas, see CPM, CPC, CPA and ROAS explained.

Customer research synthesis

Here are [number] anonymized customer comments or survey responses: [paste]. Group them into themes, name each theme, count how many responses fit it, and quote two representative responses per theme exactly as written. Then suggest messaging angles based on the top three themes.

Iterate instead of restarting

The first output is a starting point. Improve it with targeted follow-ups:

  1. Select: "Option 3 is closest. Keep its structure."
  2. Adjust: "Make the opening more concrete and cut the second sentence."
  3. Push: "Give me five more versions that are bolder than option 3."
  4. Constrain: "Rewrite at a sixth-grade reading level without losing the key benefit."
  5. Critique: "Act as a skeptical customer. What objections would this ad raise?"

The critique step is underused. Asking the model to argue against its own output often reveals weak claims and unclear phrasing.

Evaluating AI output

Before anything goes live, score it against a short rubric. Rate each item from 1 to 3, and revise anything that scores 1.

Criterion What to check
Accuracy Every claim is true and supported by information you supplied
Specificity It mentions concrete details, not generic benefits
Audience fit It speaks to the stated audience's actual problem
Voice It sounds like your brand, not a template
Clarity The main message is obvious within seconds
Action There is one clear next step
Compliance No prohibited claims for your industry or platform

For headlines and subject lines, run the top candidates through the headline analyzer as a second opinion, then test the best two with real audiences. Real performance data beats any rubric.

Guardrails to build into every prompt

Add a standing block of rules to your templates or your assistant's custom instructions:

  • Do not invent statistics, studies, quotes, reviews, or customer stories.
  • Use only product details provided in this prompt.
  • If information is missing, ask or flag it.
  • Avoid superlatives such as "best" or "guaranteed" unless I supply proof.
  • Follow US English spelling and our style guide.

Remember that you remain responsible for everything you publish. Regulated industries such as finance, health, and legal services need human review of every claim. For a fuller process covering research, drafting, and fact-checking, see our AI content workflow.

Your next step

Choose the marketing task you repeat most often, such as weekly social posts or monthly campaign reports. Write one RCTCE prompt for it using the templates above, run it three times with small variations, and score the outputs with the rubric. Save the best-performing version in a shared prompt library, and add one new template each week until your core tasks are covered.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to learn prompt engineering to get good results?

You do not need technical skills, but you do need to brief the assistant the way you would brief a capable freelancer. Clear context, specific constraints, and a good example produce most of the improvement. Advanced techniques matter far less than a well-written brief.

Why does the AI assistant keep giving me generic copy?

Generic input produces generic output. If the prompt lacks your audience, offer, proof points, and voice, the model fills gaps with the most common phrasing it knows. Add specifics and an example of copy you like, and ask for several distinct angles instead of one answer.

Is it safe to paste customer data into an AI assistant?

Check your tool's data and privacy settings and your company policy first. As a rule, remove names, emails, and any personal or confidential details before pasting data, and use business accounts that offer stronger data protections when available. When in doubt, summarize or anonymize the data.

How do I save and reuse good prompts?

Keep a shared prompt library in a document or spreadsheet with the task name, the full prompt, an example output, and notes on what to change each time. Many AI tools also let you save custom instructions or reusable templates. Review the library quarterly and retire prompts that no longer perform.
