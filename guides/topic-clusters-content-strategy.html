Topic Clusters and Pillar Pages: A Practical Content Strategy

Learn how topic clusters and pillar pages work, how to plan internal links, build an editorial calendar, refresh old posts, and measure topical authority.

GGrow.Media Editorial Team·Updated October 30, 2026·7 min read
On this pageKey takeawaysWhat a topic cluster is and why it worksChoosing your pillarsMapping your cluster contentInternal linking rules that hold a cluster togetherBuilding an editorial calendarRefreshing and consolidating contentMeasuring topical authorityYour next step

Publishing one-off articles on whatever comes to mind rarely builds lasting search traffic. A topic cluster strategy organizes your content around a few core subjects, connects related pages deliberately, and makes it obvious to readers and search engines that you cover those subjects thoroughly. Here is how to plan, build, maintain, and measure one.

Key takeaways

  • A topic cluster is a pillar page covering a broad subject, surrounded by cluster pages that each answer one specific question in depth.
  • Internal links are what turn a pile of articles into a cluster. Link the pillar to every cluster page and every cluster page back to the pillar.
  • Choose pillars that connect to your business and have enough real questions behind them to support many pages.
  • Refreshing and consolidating existing content is often faster to pay off than publishing something new.
  • Topical authority has no single metric. Track the combined impressions, queries, and clicks of each cluster over time.

What a topic cluster is and why it works

A cluster has three parts:

  1. The pillar page: a comprehensive overview of a broad topic, such as "email marketing for small businesses." It introduces each subtopic and links out to the detailed pages.
  2. Cluster pages: focused pages that each answer one specific question, such as "how to write a welcome email" or "how often to email your list."
  3. Internal links: deliberate connections between the pillar and its cluster pages, and between closely related cluster pages.

This structure helps in several ways. Readers can move naturally from overview to detail. Crawlers discover new pages quickly through the links. And covering a subject from many angles, with clear relationships between pages, makes it easier for search engines to understand what your site is about and which page best answers each query.

Choosing your pillars

Most small sites do best with three to five pillars. Evaluate each candidate against the same criteria.

Criterion Question to ask Strong signal
Business fit Does this topic lead toward what I offer? Readers of this topic are likely customers or subscribers
Depth Are there enough distinct questions? At least 8 to 10 subtopics with real search demand
Expertise Can I add first-hand insight? You've done the work, not just read about it
Competition Can a site my size compete? Some subtopics have weak or outdated results
Longevity Will people still ask about this in two years? Evergreen problems, not passing trends

A freelance photographer might choose "portrait photography," "photo editing workflow," and "running a photography business." Each connects to services or products, and each has dozens of real questions behind it.

Mapping your cluster content

Use your keyword research to fill each pillar with specific subtopics. If you haven't done that research yet, the keyword research step-by-step guide shows how.

  1. List every question and keyword related to the pillar.
  2. Group phrases that share the same intent into a single planned page.
  3. Assign each planned page to exactly one pillar.
  4. Check for overlap with pages you have already published, and decide whether to update, merge, or leave them.
  5. Define the pillar's scope: it should summarize each subtopic in a paragraph or two and link out, not repeat every detail.

Internal linking rules that hold a cluster together

Internal links are the structural core of the strategy. Follow these rules consistently:

  • Pillar to cluster: The pillar links to every cluster page, usually from the section summarizing that subtopic.
  • Cluster to pillar: Every cluster page links back to the pillar, ideally early in the content.
  • Cluster to cluster: Link sideways between closely related cluster pages where it genuinely helps the reader.
  • Descriptive anchors: Use anchor text that describes the destination, such as "batch editing portraits," instead of "read more."
  • No orphans: Every page should have at least one internal link pointing to it.
  • Update old pages: When you publish a new cluster page, go back and add links to it from the pillar and from two or three related existing pages.

Breadcrumb navigation that reflects the pillar structure reinforces these relationships for readers and crawlers alike.

Building an editorial calendar

A calendar turns the map into a schedule you can keep. Choose a cadence you can sustain for six months, whether that's one page a week or two a month. Consistency matters more than volume.

Week Piece Type Pillar Status
1 Photo editing workflow overview Pillar Editing Drafting
2 How to batch edit portraits Cluster Editing Outlined
3 Best export settings for web Cluster Editing Planned
4 Refresh: lighting basics post Update Portraits Planned

A few practical habits help:

  1. Finish one cluster before starting the next, so authority builds in one area at a time.
  2. Reserve roughly one slot in four for refreshing existing content.
  3. Batch similar tasks, such as outlining four pages in one session, to cut context switching.
  4. Draft titles early and test a few variations with the headline analyzer before you commit.

Refreshing and consolidating content

Content decays. Facts age, competitors publish better pages, and search intent shifts. Schedule refreshes when you see any of these signals:

  • Clicks declining steadily over several months
  • Rankings sitting just outside the top results, where a stronger page could move up
  • Outdated screenshots, prices, steps, or tool names
  • Two of your pages competing for the same queries

When you refresh, do real work: update facts, add missing subtopics, improve examples, tighten the introduction, and strengthen internal links. Changing only the published date without improving the content doesn't help readers and shouldn't be expected to help rankings.

When two pages overlap heavily, merge them into the stronger one, add a permanent 301 redirect from the weaker URL, and update any internal links that pointed to it.

Measuring topical authority

There is no official topical authority score. Instead, track proxies at the cluster level, not just page by page.

Metric How to check What improvement looks like
Queries per cluster Search Console, filtered by the cluster's URLs More distinct queries over time
Cluster impressions Search Console performance report Steady growth across the whole group
Average position Search Console, same filter Gradual upward movement across pages
Pages earning clicks Share of cluster pages with at least some clicks Rising percentage
Internal journeys Analytics paths from pillar to cluster pages Readers visiting multiple pages per session

Review these quarterly. For a deeper framework on choosing and tracking metrics, see the growth KPIs and analytics guide. Expect early movement in impressions and query counts well before clicks rise.

Your next step

Pick one pillar topic today. List at least ten specific questions under it, group them into planned pages, and check which ones you already cover. Put the pillar and its first three cluster pages on your calendar for the next month, and block one session to add internal links between everything you have already published on that topic.

Frequently asked questions

How many cluster pages does a pillar need?

There is no fixed number, but many sites start with roughly 6 to 12 supporting pages per pillar and expand from there. The right count is however many distinct questions your audience has about the topic. Stop when new pages would only repeat existing ones.

Should the pillar page be published first?

Either order works, but publishing the pillar alongside the first few cluster pages gives you something to link to immediately. A pillar with no supporting pages is just a long article, and cluster pages with no pillar lack a clear hub.

What is keyword cannibalization?

It happens when two or more of your pages target the same search intent and end up competing with each other. The usual fix is to merge them into one stronger page, redirect the weaker URL, and update internal links to point at the survivor.

How often should I refresh old content?

Review your most important pages at least twice a year and the rest annually. Refresh sooner when a page loses clicks, when facts or tools change, or when it ranks just outside the top results and could be pushed higher.
