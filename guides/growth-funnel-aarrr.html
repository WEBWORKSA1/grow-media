The AARRR Growth Funnel: Find and Fix Your Leakiest Stage

Use the AARRR growth funnel to pick metrics for each stage, find your leakiest stage and prioritize growth experiments with simple ICE scoring.

GGrow.Media Editorial Team·Updated October 30, 2026·8 min read
On this pageKey takeawaysThe five stages, definedChoosing metrics for each stageMapping your funnel with real numbersFinding the leakiest stageBuilding an experiments backlogRunning the monthly growth reviewYour next step

Growth problems often feel vague: sales are flat, the audience is not growing, something is off. The AARRR framework turns that fog into five measurable stages so you can see exactly where people drop out. This guide shows you how to define each stage for your business, find the leakiest one, and run a disciplined backlog of experiments to fix it.

Key takeaways

  • AARRR breaks growth into Acquisition, Activation, Retention, Referral, and Revenue.
  • Define one primary metric per stage, using actions that matter to your specific business.
  • Compare stage-to-stage conversion rates to find the leakiest stage, then focus there.
  • Score experiment ideas with ICE (Impact, Confidence, Ease) to decide what to test first.
  • Run a few experiments at a time, document every result, and review the funnel monthly.

The five stages, defined

Each stage asks a simple question about the people moving through your business.

Stage Question Small business example Creator example
Acquisition How do people find you? Website visitors from search and ads New viewers or profile visits
Activation Do they have a good first experience? Requests a quote or creates an account Subscribes, follows, or joins the email list
Retention Do they come back? Makes a second purchase Returns to watch or opens the next email
Referral Do they tell others? Refers a friend or leaves a review Shares a post or recommends the channel
Revenue Do they pay you? Completes a purchase or subscription Buys a product, joins a membership

The order is a guide, not a law. Revenue can happen at the first visit for an online store, and referral can come before purchase. What matters is that each stage is defined clearly enough to measure.

Choosing metrics for each stage

Pick one primary metric per stage, plus one or two supporting metrics. The primary metric should be an action that predicts value, not just activity.

  1. Acquisition: new visitors or new leads by channel. Supporting metrics include cost per visitor for paid channels and share of traffic by source.
  2. Activation: percentage of new visitors who complete your key first action. Define that action carefully; for a software tool, it might be completing setup, not just signing up.
  3. Retention: percentage of activated users or customers who return within a set window, such as 30 or 90 days.
  4. Referral: referrals, reviews, or shares per 100 customers.
  5. Revenue: conversion to paid, average order value, and revenue per customer.

Finding your activation moment

Activation is the stage most businesses define poorly. Look at customers who stayed and customers who left, and ask what the stayers did early that the leavers did not. Common answers include completing a first project, attending a first session, or reading several pieces of content in one week. That action becomes your activation metric.

Mapping your funnel with real numbers

Pull 30 or 90 days of data and build a simple table. Here is an illustrative example for a small online course business:

Stage Count Conversion from previous stage
Visitors (Acquisition) 10,000 Not applicable
Email signups (Activation) 400 4.0%
Opened 3+ emails in 30 days (Retention) 160 40%
Purchased a course (Revenue) 12 7.5%
Referred someone or reviewed (Referral) 3 25%

Enter your own stage counts into the funnel conversion calculator to calculate each step's rate and overall conversion automatically.

Finding the leakiest stage

The leakiest stage is not always the one with the lowest percentage. A 4% signup rate might be normal for your traffic, while a 40% retention rate might be weak for an engaged email list. Use three lenses:

  1. Benchmark against yourself: compare each rate to its own history. A stage that has declined is a strong candidate.
  2. Compare against reasonable expectations: commonly cited ranges for your industry and channel can help, but treat them as rough signals, since they vary widely by audience and offer.
  3. Estimate the upside: calculate what happens to final revenue if you improve each stage by the same relative amount, such as 20%.

In the example above, raising email signups from 4% to 4.8% adds 80 signups at the top. But because every later rate applies to that larger pool, the gain flows all the way through. Run this "what if" for each stage and you will usually find one where a realistic improvement produces the biggest downstream effect.

Fix retention before scaling acquisition

If people activate and then disappear, more traffic only increases the number of people who leave. A weak retention stage is often the most valuable fix, because it raises the value of everything above it.

Building an experiments backlog

Once you know which stage to focus on, generate as many ideas as you can for improving it. Then prioritize them with ICE scoring.

ICE scoring

Score each idea from 1 to 10 on three dimensions:

  • Impact: how much will this move the target metric if it works?
  • Confidence: how sure are you it will work, based on evidence such as customer feedback or past tests?
  • Ease: how quickly and cheaply can you run it?

Average the three scores (some teams multiply them instead) and sort the list.

Idea (Activation stage) Impact Confidence Ease ICE score
Add a checklist lead magnet to top blog posts 7 6 8 7.0
Shorten the signup form to email only 5 7 9 7.0
Rewrite homepage headline around the main outcome 6 5 9 6.7
Build an interactive quiz for new visitors 8 4 3 5.0

ICE is subjective, and that is fine. Its value is in forcing explicit trade-offs and stopping the loudest idea from winning by default. For activation ideas specifically, our guide to lead magnets and list building offers plenty of starting points.

Writing a good experiment card

For each experiment you run, write down:

  1. Hypothesis: "If we shorten the signup form, signups will increase because visitors abandon long forms."
  2. Metric: the single number you will judge it by.
  3. Duration or sample: how long it will run or how many visitors it needs.
  4. Result: what happened, with numbers.
  5. Decision: keep, discard, or iterate.

Store cards in one shared document so learning accumulates over time.

Running the monthly growth review

Hold a short review each month:

  1. Update the funnel table with the latest numbers.
  2. Note any stage that moved meaningfully, up or down.
  3. Review completed experiments and record decisions.
  4. Confirm or change the focus stage.
  5. Pick the next one to three experiments from the backlog.

To decide which numbers belong on your dashboard between reviews, read our guide to growth KPIs and analytics.

Your next step

Write a one-line definition for each of the five stages for your business today, including the specific activation action. Pull the last 90 days of data, fill in the funnel table, and calculate each stage's conversion rate. Identify your leakiest stage, brainstorm ten ideas to fix it, score them with ICE, and launch the top-scoring experiment this week.

Frequently asked questions

Why is it called the pirate funnel?

The first letters of the five stages, Acquisition, Activation, Retention, Referral, and Revenue, spell AARRR, which sounds like a stereotypical pirate's growl. The nickname stuck because it makes the framework easy to remember. Some versions add Awareness at the front, making it AAARRR.

Does the AARRR funnel work for creators, not just startups?

Yes. A creator's acquisition might be new viewers, activation a first subscription or signup, retention returning viewers or openers, referral shares, and revenue memberships, products, or sponsorships. The stages translate to almost any business with an audience.

Which stage should I focus on first?

Usually retention, if it is weak. Pouring more traffic into a funnel that loses people after their first experience multiplies waste. Once people stick around, improvements to acquisition and revenue compound instead of leaking away.

How many experiments should I run at once?

For a small team, one to three at a time is usually plenty. Running too many makes it hard to tell which change caused which result, especially with modest traffic. Finish, measure, and document each test before starting the next batch.
