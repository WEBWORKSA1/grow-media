Email Deliverability Basics: Stay Out of Spam, Land in the Inbox

Email deliverability basics explained plainly: set up SPF, DKIM, and DMARC, keep your list clean, reduce spam complaints, and warm up a new sending domain.

GGrow.Media Editorial Team·Updated October 30, 2026·7 min read

You can write the best email of your life and still have it vanish into a spam folder. Deliverability is the set of technical and behavioral signals that tell mailbox providers your messages are wanted. The good news is that most of it is straightforward once someone explains it plainly. This guide covers authentication, list quality, engagement, current bulk-sender expectations, spam complaints, and how to warm up a new domain.

Key takeaways

  • Delivery means a server accepted your email. Deliverability means it reached the inbox. Aim for the second.
  • SPF, DKIM, and DMARC prove your emails really come from you. Set up all three on your own domain.
  • Mailbox providers watch how recipients behave. Engagement and complaints shape your sender reputation.
  • Major providers now publish requirements for bulk senders, including authentication, easy unsubscribes, and low complaint rates.
  • New domains need a gradual warm-up. Start with your most engaged subscribers and increase volume slowly.

What affects deliverability

Mailbox providers weigh several groups of signals when deciding where to place your email:

  1. Authentication: Can they verify the email truly came from your domain?
  2. Sender reputation: What is the history of your domain and sending IP address?
  3. Recipient engagement: Do people open, click, reply, or move your emails out of spam? Or do they delete without reading and report spam?
  4. List quality: Are you sending to real, consenting, active addresses?
  5. Content and formatting: Does the message look like legitimate email, with working links and a clear sender?

No single factor guarantees the inbox, but weakness in any one of them can send you to spam.

SPF, DKIM, and DMARC explained plainly

Think of these three records as a verification system for your domain. They live in your domain's DNS settings, and your email platform gives you the exact values to add.

SPF (Sender Policy Framework)

SPF is a published list of the servers allowed to send email for your domain. When a message arrives, the receiving server checks whether it came from a server on that list.

  • You can have only one SPF record per domain. Combine multiple services into a single record.
  • SPF is limited to 10 DNS lookups. Adding too many services can break it.

DKIM (DomainKeys Identified Mail)

DKIM adds a digital signature to each email. Your platform signs outgoing messages with a private key, and receivers check the signature against a public key published in your DNS. If the message was altered in transit, or wasn't signed by an authorized sender, the check fails.

DMARC (Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting, and Conformance)

DMARC tells receivers what to do when an email fails authentication, and it requests reports so you can see who is sending as your domain. It also requires alignment, meaning the domain in the visible "From" address must match the domain verified by SPF or DKIM.

Record What it answers Typical setup
SPF Is this server allowed to send for this domain? One TXT record listing your sending services
DKIM Was this message signed by the domain and left unaltered? Record values supplied by your email platform
DMARC What should happen if checks fail, and where should reports go? TXT record at _dmarc.yourdomain.com

A safe DMARC rollout

  1. Start with a monitoring policy, such as v=DMARC1; p=none; rua=mailto:reports@example.com.
  2. Review reports for a few weeks to confirm all your legitimate services pass.
  3. Move to p=quarantine, which sends failing mail to spam.
  4. Once you're confident, move to p=reject, which blocks failing mail entirely.

What mailbox providers expect from bulk senders

Since 2024, Gmail and Yahoo have enforced requirements for bulk senders, generally described as those sending around 5,000 or more messages a day to their users, and Microsoft has announced similar rules for its consumer mail services. Requirements evolve, so check each provider's current sender guidelines, but the core expectations are consistent:

  • Authenticate with SPF and DKIM, and publish a DMARC record (at least p=none).
  • Align the "From" domain with your authenticated domain.
  • Include one-click unsubscribe in marketing emails and honor requests within two days.
  • Keep reported spam complaint rates below 0.3%, ideally well under 0.1%.
  • Send from a domain you own rather than a free webmail address.

Even if you send far less, treat these as best practice. They are the clearest public signal of what providers consider trustworthy behavior.

List quality: send only to people who want it

The fastest way to damage your reputation is sending to people who didn't ask for your email.

  • Earn consent clearly. Explain what subscribers will receive and how often.
  • Consider double opt-in. Confirmation filters out typos, fake addresses, and bots.
  • Never buy, rent, or scrape lists. These often contain spam traps, addresses that exist solely to catch senders with poor practices.
  • Remove hard bounces at once. Repeatedly sending to invalid addresses signals a poorly maintained list.
  • Sunset inactive subscribers. Run a short re-engagement series for people who haven't engaged in roughly three to six months, then remove those who don't respond.

Engagement signals and how to improve them

Providers watch what recipients do with your messages. Positive signals include opens, clicks, replies, adding you to contacts, and moving your email out of spam. Negative signals include deleting without opening, ignoring messages for months, and reporting spam.

Open rates are an imperfect measure because some email apps load messages automatically. Lean more on clicks and replies. To improve engagement:

  1. Send content that matches what you promised at signup.
  2. Use a consistent, recognizable sender name.
  3. Invite replies with a genuine question.
  4. Send your most engaged segment first when testing new content or volume.
  5. Track link clicks consistently by tagging links with the UTM builder, so you can see which emails drive real visits.

Reducing spam complaints

People usually click "report spam" because they don't recognize you, you email too often, or unsubscribing looks difficult. Fix the cause:

Cause Fix
Subscriber doesn't recognize you Use a consistent sender name and remind them why they joined
Too many emails Set expectations at signup and offer a lower-frequency option
Unsubscribe is hard to find Place a clear unsubscribe link in every email and enable one-click unsubscribe
Content doesn't match the promise Revisit your signup page and welcome emails
Old, cold addresses Re-engage or remove subscribers who haven't engaged in months

Google Postmaster Tools lets you monitor reputation and complaint rates for mail sent to Gmail users once your volume is high enough to register.

Warming up a new domain

A brand-new sending domain has no reputation. Sending a large volume immediately looks suspicious. Warm up gradually. The schedule below is an illustrative starting point; adjust it to your platform's guidance and your results.

Week Who to send to Approximate daily volume
1 Most engaged subscribers only A few dozen to a few hundred
2 Engaged subscribers from the last 30 to 60 days Roughly double week one
3 Add subscribers active in the last 90 days Increase again if metrics stay healthy
4 and beyond Full active list Scale gradually toward normal volume

Most small senders use a shared IP address managed by their email platform, which already has an established reputation. Dedicated IP addresses generally only make sense at high, consistent volume. Some businesses also send marketing email from a subdomain, such as news.example.com, to keep it separate from their everyday business email.

Your next step

Open your email platform's domain settings today and confirm that SPF, DKIM, and DMARC are set up and passing. If DMARC is missing, add a monitoring policy now. Then check that every campaign includes a visible unsubscribe link, and schedule a re-engagement series for subscribers who haven't clicked in the past six months.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between delivery and deliverability?

Delivery means the receiving mail server accepted your message. Deliverability means it reached the inbox rather than the spam folder or a filtered tab. An email can be delivered and still go unseen, which is why inbox placement is the goal.

Do I need SPF, DKIM, and DMARC if my list is small?

Yes. The strictest requirements from major mailbox providers apply to high-volume senders, but authentication helps every sender prove legitimacy. Setting all three up early is quick, and it prevents problems as your list grows.

What spam complaint rate is too high?

Major mailbox providers have said bulk senders should stay below a 0.3% complaint rate, and many deliverability practitioners recommend aiming under 0.1%. If you approach those levels, review how subscribers joined, how often you send, and how easy it is to unsubscribe.

Why are my emails suddenly going to spam?

Common causes include a broken or missing authentication record, a spike in complaints or bounces, a sudden jump in sending volume, or sending to long-inactive addresses. Check your authentication first, then review recent changes to your list and sending pattern.
