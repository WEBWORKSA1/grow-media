Google Ads for Beginners: Build and Launch Your First Campaign

Google Ads for beginners: structure your account, choose campaign and match types, pick a bid strategy, write ads, track conversions, and plan month one.

GGrow.Media Editorial Team·Updated October 30, 2026·8 min read
On this pageKey takeawaysHow a Google Ads account is structuredChoosing a campaign typeKeywords and match typesBidding strategiesWriting effective ad copyLanding pages that convertSetting up conversion trackingA first-month budget planYour next step

Google Ads lets you appear in front of people at the exact moment they search for what you offer. That intent makes it powerful, and it also makes it easy to waste money if your account is set up loosely. This guide walks you through account structure, campaign types, keywords, bidding, ad copy, landing pages, conversion tracking, and a first-month budget plan.

Key takeaways

  • Structure drives control. Group tightly related keywords into ad groups with ads and landing pages written specifically for them.
  • Start with a Search campaign. It offers the most transparency and reaches people who are already looking.
  • Match types and negative keywords decide which searches you pay for. Review your search terms report often.
  • Set up conversion tracking before you spend meaningfully. Without it, automated bidding is guessing.
  • Treat the first month as paid research. Measure, prune, and refine before scaling.

How a Google Ads account is structured

An account is organized in layers, and each layer controls different settings.

  1. Account: billing, users, and account-wide conversion settings.
  2. Campaign: budget, bid strategy, locations, languages, networks, and schedule.
  3. Ad group: a tightly themed set of keywords.
  4. Ads and keywords: the ads that show and the searches you target.

Keep each ad group focused on one theme so the ad and landing page can speak directly to that search. Here is an example for a local dog grooming business:

Campaign Ad group Example keywords Landing page
Grooming services Full groom "dog grooming near me," [dog grooming] Full groom service page
Grooming services Nail trimming "dog nail trim," [dog nail clipping] Nail trim page
Grooming services Puppy grooming "puppy first groom," [puppy grooming] Puppy package page

Choosing a campaign type

Campaign type Where ads appear Best for Beginner fit
Search Google search results Capturing active demand Best starting point
Performance Max Across Google's channels from one campaign Advertisers with solid conversion data Better after you have history
Display Websites and apps in Google's network Visual awareness and remarketing Use with care; easy to overspend
Video YouTube Awareness, consideration, storytelling Good if you have strong video
Demand Gen YouTube, Discover, Gmail Visual demand creation Intermediate
Shopping Product listings with images and prices Ecommerce stores Good for stores with a product feed

Start with Search. You'll see exactly what people typed before clicking, which teaches you more about your market than any other campaign type.

Keywords and match types

Match types control how closely a search must relate to your keyword.

Match type Syntax Behavior
Broad dog grooming Matches searches related to the meaning, including ones without your words
Phrase "dog grooming" Matches searches that include the meaning of your phrase
Exact [dog grooming] Matches searches with the same meaning or intent

All three match types include close variants such as plurals, misspellings, and synonyms. Beginners often get the most control by starting with phrase and exact match, then testing broad match once conversion tracking and automated bidding are working well.

Negative keywords

Negative keywords stop your ads from showing on irrelevant searches. The dog groomer above might add "jobs," "course," "salary," and "DIY." Review the search terms report every few days at first and add negatives for anything that isn't a potential customer.

Bidding strategies

Bidding tells Google how to spend your budget.

  • Manual CPC: you set maximum cost-per-click bids. Maximum control, more work.
  • Maximize clicks: gets as many clicks as possible within budget. Useful briefly for gathering early data; consider setting a maximum CPC limit.
  • Maximize conversions: uses automated bidding to get the most conversions within budget. Requires working conversion tracking.
  • Target CPA: aims for an average cost per acquisition you set.
  • Maximize conversion value and Target ROAS: optimize for revenue rather than conversion count, best for stores with varied order values.

A common path is to start with Maximize conversions once tracking is verified, then move to Target CPA after the campaign has a steady flow of conversions. Practitioners often cite around 30 or more conversions a month as a comfortable threshold, though that varies. If terms like CPA and ROAS are unfamiliar, read CPM, CPC, CPA, and ROAS explained.

Writing effective ad copy

Responsive search ads let you enter up to 15 headlines of up to 30 characters each and up to 4 descriptions of up to 90 characters. Google tests combinations automatically.

Cover these angles across your headlines:

  1. The keyword theme: "Dog Grooming in Austin"
  2. A core benefit: "Calm, Stress-Free Grooming"
  3. Proof or specifics: "Certified Groomers Since 2015"
  4. An offer: "First Nail Trim Free"
  5. A call to action: "Book Online in 60 Seconds"

Only use claims that are true for your business. Then add assets, which make your ad larger and more useful: sitelinks to key pages, callouts for short benefits, structured snippets for service lists, and call or location assets for local businesses.

Landing pages that convert

The click is only half the job. Your landing page should:

  • Match the ad's message and keyword closely
  • Load quickly and work well on mobile
  • Focus on one primary action, such as booking, calling, or buying
  • Show trust signals, such as genuine reviews, guarantees, and clear contact details
  • Make the call to action visible without excessive scrolling

Google's Quality Score, rated from 1 to 10, reflects expected click-through rate, ad relevance, and landing page experience. Better scores can lower your costs and improve your ad position.

Setting up conversion tracking

Conversion tracking tells you which keywords and ads produce real results.

  1. Decide what counts as a conversion: purchases, lead forms, phone calls, or bookings.
  2. Install the Google tag on your site, or import conversions from Google Analytics.
  3. Set your most valuable actions as primary conversions and supporting actions as secondary.
  4. Consider enhanced conversions, which can improve measurement accuracy.
  5. Test every conversion by completing it yourself and confirming it registers.

Avoid optimizing toward weak signals such as page views. Automated bidding will deliver exactly what you measure.

A first-month budget plan

Estimate your budget with a simple formula:

Daily budget = expected cost per click × clicks needed per day

If clicks in your market commonly cost around $2 and you want about 20 clicks a day, plan for roughly $40 a day. Costs vary widely by industry and location, from under a dollar to well over $10 per click, so check the keyword planner's estimates for your terms. Plug your numbers into the ad metrics calculator to see what conversion rate you'd need to hit a target cost per acquisition.

Week Focus Actions
1 Launch and watch Check search terms daily, add negatives, confirm conversions fire
2 Prune waste Pause keywords with spend and no relevant clicks, refine match types
3 Improve ads Replace weak headlines, add assets, review landing page behavior
4 Evaluate Compare cost per conversion to your target and decide what to scale or cut

Your next step

Write down the single action you want visitors to take, then set up conversion tracking for it before creating any campaign. Next, build one Search campaign with two or three tightly themed ad groups, a starter list of negative keywords, and one landing page per theme. Launch with a budget you're comfortable treating as research, and review search terms daily during the first week.

Frequently asked questions

How much should I spend on Google Ads as a beginner?

Start with a budget you can afford to spend on learning, often a few hundred dollars for the first month in lower-cost industries and more in competitive ones. Estimate it by multiplying your expected cost per click by the number of clicks you need to reach a meaningful number of conversions.

Which campaign type should beginners start with?

A Search campaign is usually the best first step because you choose the keywords, see exactly which searches trigger your ads, and reach people who are actively looking. Automated campaign types work better once you have reliable conversion data.

What is a good Quality Score?

Quality Score is rated from 1 to 10 at the keyword level, based on expected click-through rate, ad relevance, and landing page experience. Scores of 7 or higher are generally considered healthy. Treat it as a diagnostic, not a goal in itself.

How long before I know if my campaign is working?

Give a new campaign at least two to four weeks and enough clicks to produce meaningful data before making big judgments. Check search terms and obvious waste daily at first, but avoid rebuilding the campaign based on a few days of results.
